Christa Pike, the well-known convicted murderer on death row, is one of nearly 10,000 people given the death sentence since 1972. Her failed execution on September 30 brought the question of justified death sentences back into focus, but many people don’t know much about the death sentence besides its main purpose: to kill people who have committed extreme crimes.

Capital crimes, terrorism, genocide, murder, war crimes, and treason are all offences punishable by death. At the moment, 53 countries, including the United States, allow the death penalty, while 111 have abolished it, and 23 haven’t had an execution in more than 10 years. Around half of the world’s population lives in countries where the death penalty is allowed, with the frequency of US executions matched by Saudi Arabia, Yemen, North Korea, Iran, and China, according to the Center for Constitutional Rights.

With the world so divided on the implementation of the death penalty, it’s important to take a look at both opinions and arguments.

Some say the death penalty is a general deterrent. In an analysis, economist Isaac Ehrlich found that 7 lives were spared for every execution, and although many people challenged this theory, other studies have found similar results. The death penalty is also a specific deterrent, meaning whoever was killed through the death penalty will not be alive to commit that crime again. The Trump White House also deemed the death penalty a key deterrent and the only proper punishment for heinous crimes. 

Others have found that the death penalty, which costs 2 to 5 times more than life in prison, doesn't work. In fact, criminologists have found that the death penalty *increases* the likelihood of murder by brutalizing society, and this is reinforced by comparing states and countries without the death penalty— which had low crime rates— to states and countries with it— which had higher crime rates. The argument for determent is made nearly useless when it’s considered that most murders are committed impulsively. Determent is not a valid argument towards the death penalty.

Representatives from the Death Penalty Information Center say the risk of innocent people getting executed is also extremely low, and with safeguards now implemented, there has been no evidence of this happening since the 1970s. This is especially true because of the system of appeals, allowing the case to be reviewed time after time after time. Although improvements can be made, it’s not justified to remove this necessary process, one which is legal in the federal government, military, and 29 states, just because of a small risk of error. Along with this, the Supreme Court says the death penalty is constitutional, and society has a right to punish those who have committed heinous crimes.

Columbia University, however, found that over 80% of the defendants' court cases, when retried, were not convicted of murder, and with the Geneva Convention finding life on death row as a violation of its rules, it’s imperative to avoid wrongful convictions. “The death row phenomenon,” life on death row, has two main aspects. One is reaching the day of execution multiple times, which violates the International Human Rights Law prohibiting torture. The other is solitary confinement. Of the 23-year average wait time on death row, half of that time is spent in solitary confinement. The combination of decades in isolation and the constant knowledge of impending execution is, according to Human Rights Law,  legal torture in a country where torture is illegal.
