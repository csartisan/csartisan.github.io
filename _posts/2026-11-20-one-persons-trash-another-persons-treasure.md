Imagine finding a Dior handbag for five dollars in a rack full of old purses, or a Picasso piece in a pile of used furniture for three dollars. Today, thrifting and secondhand shopping have risen from a financial necessity to a global trend.

One of the reasons thrifting has become so popular is because thrift stores and secondhand marketplaces often hold vintage and sometimes even valuable underpriced goods. Recently, older fashion trends have resurfaced, such as UGGs and older Hollister pieces. Most thrift stores carry pieces like these, making them a popular shopping destination for teens. “I prefer shopping secondhand because I feel like you can’t rely on actual stores to get things that are timeless. Timeless things and really awesome things are at the thrift. Also, it’s a lot cheaper secondhand. It’s *so* much cheaper,” said Ariella Dawson, an avid secondhand shopper. However, these amazing pieces and amazing prices at thrift stores have started to change. The rise in thrifting has also cultivated the rise in resellers, people who snag good deals at the thrift and resell them for higher prices on sites like Depop and eBay. Ariella said that this has become one of the cons of thrifting. “People are very competitive when it comes to finding good clothes, especially on Depop”. Some argue that resellers save clothing pieces from the thrift store and make fashion more accessible to others, while others argue that they are profit-driven and make secondhand marketplaces hostile. 

Before thrifting became a trend, secondhand shopping was seen as a last resort and reserved only for the frugal. People didn’t thrift in search of designer finds or coveted vintage–they searched for cheap clothes. This used to be a major benefit of thrifting, you could find basic shirts or everyday household goods for under $5. However, with the rise of thrifting and reselling, prices have begun rising to the point where those who need cheap clothes can’t afford them. Many people have taken to social media to complain about this surge in prices, showing everyday basics priced unreasonably. Used Carhartt and Patagonia jackets used to be a staple for the working class, but due to high demand from consumers, thrift stores have started pricing them close to retail value (~$50). This surge in prices has also frustrated many secondhand shoppers, because most thrift stores, such as Goodwill, receive the clothes they sell for free as donations. 

One of the many benefits of secondhand shopping is environmental sustainability. 11.3 million tons of textile waste are dumped into landfills annually, meaning over 2,000 pieces of clothing are wasted each second.  Purchasing a single secondhand item instead of a new one saves roughly 8.41 pounds of carbon dioxide emissions. For context, that’s the equivalent of driving around 10 miles in a gas-powered car. The fashion industry accounts for 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions and 20% of global wastewater emissions. By shopping secondhand, you avoid contributing to the fashion industry’s growing carbon footprint and move perfectly good clothing from landfills to closets. Thrifting also diverts your money towards smaller corporations, especially if you thrift locally, in markets run by churches, hospitals, or shelters. “Thrifting has made me feel a lot better about buying clothes because it’s cheaper and more ethical because I know I’m not funding these organizations that aren’t very good,” Ariella Dawson said.

Thrifting has made a massive impact on today’s fashion, and has changed from a social stigma to a global trend in sustainability. It is important that thrifting remains accessible, continues to protect the environment, and supports local communities.
