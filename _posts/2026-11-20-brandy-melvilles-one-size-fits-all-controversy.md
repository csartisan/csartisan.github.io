One size fits all - or does it? For a brand as popular with teenagers as Brandy Melville’s, the company’s signature “one size fits all” approach raises an important question: Who actually gets to fit in?

Brandy Melville has been one of the most popular clothing stores for teenagers for almost 20 years. You will most likely spot someone wearing one of their tank tops or a pair of sweatpants daily. But when Leah Borboa tried on her Brandy flare pants for the first time, she loved them. 

“In 2020, I loved the first pair of pants I tried on, and I wore them all the time. They looked pretty, and they made me feel pretty.” 

Every time you see a company trying to conform its clothing sizes, it’s hard not to think of people who don’t buy its items because they are physically unable to fit the standards. 

“As I got older, I stopped wearing their clothes because the fit made me uncomfortable. I talked to my mom and found clothes that made me feel better about my body.”

 As a brand that holds significant influence over younger generations, its narrow perception of acceptable body sizes harms students’ self-esteem and body image. 

“The way that Brandy Melville has their ‘one size fits all’ mentality makes it so everyone loses,” Sophomore Leia VanAckeren said. The unrealistic manufacturing world they try to create sets the expectation that an extremely small body should be the standard for everyone else. Pressuring teenagers to conform to an unrealistic so-called perfect size at such a formative age can foster negative thinking and eating habits. 

“I wear all their clothes, but a lot of the time it’s not a flattering cut.” Sophomore Trista Wu said. She continued by talking about how putting on the clothes makes her feel awkward: “They tend to be made for people who are smaller-chested, and their shirts have very small-fitted, or tall shoulders.” 

The constant comparison to unrealistic body standards through marketing and fashion can lead to anxiety, depression, constant body comparison, and low self-esteem. Maintaining this mindset promotes dangerous behaviors such as disordered eating, overexercising, and, most importantly, body dysmorphia.
